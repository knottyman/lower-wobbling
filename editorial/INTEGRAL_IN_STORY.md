# INTEGRAL in the Story

## Purpose

This document records the core principles of the INTEGRAL framework that are relevant to the development of *The Greenhouse at Ballywobble*. It is an editorial reference for the author and writing agents: it helps us bring the framework into the villagers' decisions, working arrangements and use of technology without turning the novel into a technical manual or manifesto.

It is not a replacement for the story bible. **`STORY.md` remains the authority for established plot, character and story canon.** This document describes the framework and the direction in which the story may explore it. Where an idea here would change an established story mechanism, the author must decide before the prose or canon is changed.

## Source and scope

The immediate source for this document is **`editorial/Integral_Plain_Language_Guide.pdf`**, a nine-page plain-English guide to Peter Joseph's *INTEGRAL* technical white paper, v0.1 (December 2025). The guide describes the paper as a 342-page technical document.

This document therefore records what that plain-English guide says and draws out its implications for the novel. It is **not a claim that we have independently checked every detail against the full technical specification**. Where the guide does not provide implementation detail, do not invent it or present a guess as a feature of INTEGRAL. The full paper should be consulted if a future scene depends on technical behaviour that the guide does not explain.

## The central idea

INTEGRAL is a proposed way of organising an economy around cooperation, shared knowledge, democratic decision-making and the measurement of real resources and impacts, rather than money, prices and corporate competition.

The guide describes a system intended to:
- record real work, material use and environmental impact;
- make designs and improvements openly available;
- let people affected by decisions participate in shaping them;
- coordinate production and resources cooperatively;
- monitor outcomes and send feedback so the system can correct course;
- begin voluntarily in small groups and connect those groups into larger networks without creating a central authority.

This is not a claim that everything becomes unlimited or that scarcity disappears overnight. The guide describes a gradual trajectory: better designs, coordination and reduced waste can lower the effort needed to meet needs over time.

For the novel, the important dramatic question is not whether a character can explain this vision. It is what changes when people have practical ways to make it work — and what they discover when those ways meet real people, constraints and disagreements.

## The five connected systems

The guide describes five systems. They have distinct responsibilities, but none is meant to operate alone.

### 1. CDS — Collaborative Decision System

**Purpose in the framework:** collective decision-making.

The guide says the CDS allows people to submit proposals, concerns and objections; organises contributions so areas of agreement and disagreement can be seen; and allows degrees of support rather than reducing every response to a simple yes/no vote. It looks for broad agreement and flags matters needing more discussion. For complex disagreements, it can convene a structured dialogue called a Syntegrity session. Decisions and the reasons behind them are recorded.

**Story implications:**
- A proposal should be visible and open to response, including concerns from people who are not the loudest in the room.
- Agreement is not the same as everyone having their first preference. A concern may lead to a revision; a fundamental objection may mean the proposal needs more work.
- The process should make disagreement useful rather than treating it as disloyalty or an obstacle to be silenced.
- Records help people understand what was decided and why, and allow a decision to be revisited with context.

The villagers' existing **Board** and its two-mode annoyance gauge are established story canon. They can be treated as an early, local decision-making practice with some family resemblance to the CDS, **not as proof that Ballywobble already has the complete CDS described in the guide**. Preserve the existing gauge rules in `STORY.md`; do not add new modes or quietly replace its consent process.

### 2. OAD — Open Access Design

**Purpose in the framework:** shared, continually improved designs and practical knowledge.

The guide describes an open library of designs, instructions and blueprints. Designs can be improved by anyone, and improvements are shared so other communities can use them. A design records more than how to make something: it also considers lifespan, repair, materials and environmental impact. Knowledge is treated as a shared resource rather than locked behind patents or proprietary restrictions.

**Story implications:**
- The village need not invent every tool, process or piece of infrastructure from scratch. People can find, adapt and improve work already done elsewhere.
- A design is not just a drawing. Questions of repair, durability, materials, energy and environmental cost matter.
- When a local improvement is shared, another group can benefit — and may improve it again.
- Shared designs create opportunities for exchange between communities without requiring them to organise themselves identically.

Ines's **Notebook** already records materials, energy, labour and environmental cost. It is a useful story foothold for this principle, but it is not yet the full shared, searchable and version-controlled OAD library described by the guide. Keep that distinction clear.

### 3. ITC — Integral Time Credits

**Purpose in the framework:** recording contribution and organising access without turning credits into money.

The guide explicitly says ITC is **not a new currency**. Work contributions are recorded in hours, with adjustments for the nature or difficulty of work. Access to community-produced goods or services uses credits from a person's record. The guide says access values take account of labour, material and environmental impact, and urgency or fairness signals. Credits are used rather than accumulated indefinitely: they cannot be traded or lent, and unused credits fade over time. Access to essentials is guaranteed regardless of a person's capacity to work.

**Story implications:**
- Contribution and access can be recorded transparently without creating a market in credits.
- A record of contribution must not become a measure of a person's worth.
- People who cannot contribute in the same way or to the same extent must not be excluded from essentials.
- The environmental and material costs of production matter alongside labour.
- Preventing the accumulation, exchange or lending of credits is part of preventing credits from becoming a source of power over other people.

**Important canon distinction:** Ballywobble's existing **Ledger** is a local time-credit arrangement with its own established rules: credits fade, are not money, and cannot convert to euros. **Odd Jobs** handles ordinary euro-facing purchases and payments; the one-way valve must remain intact. The Ledger has some principles in common with the ITC described in the guide, but do not assume that its current mechanics implement the full ITC model — including its complete access-value calculations or all its safeguards. Any move towards those mechanics is a development decision for the author, not an automatic retcon.

### 4. COS — Cooperative Organization System

**Purpose in the framework:** coordinating the work and resources needed to produce things.

The guide describes COS as organising cooperative work groups around the skills a project needs; scheduling tasks; tracking materials and resources; and coordinating supplies from other communities or, when necessary, from outside the network. Production records include work, skills, materials and tool wear. The guide presents this as transparent coordination rather than command by bosses.

**Story implications:**
- A decision to do something must be connected to the practical question of who has the skills, time, tools and materials to do it.
- Coordination should make commitments and dependencies visible without turning a volunteer into someone's subordinate.
- Shortages and outside purchases are real conditions to plan around, not embarrassing failures to hide.
- Production records can inform other parts of the system, including the calculation of access values.
- The work should be organised; it should not depend on one heroic person remembering everything.

The village's **Rota**, work groups and Odd Jobs arrangements are possible early examples of cooperative coordination. They are not yet a full COS. Preserve the story's distinction between a list that helps people coordinate and a person who has authority over them.

### 5. FRS — Feedback & Review System

**Purpose in the framework:** monitoring whether the system is working and directing problems to the part that can respond.

The guide says the FRS monitors ecological impacts and limits, fairness of workloads, equitable access to goods, production bottlenecks and waste, the accuracy of ITC values, and whether communities in the network are sharing fairly. When it detects a problem, it sends a signal to the relevant system: for example, a design issue to OAD, a workload or access issue to the appropriate process, or a shortage to production coordination.

**Story implications:**
- The group needs to notice the consequences of its decisions, not simply record that a decision was made.
- Feedback should be timely enough to change what happens next.
- Different problems need to reach the people or process able to address them.
- Monitoring should include ecological limits, workload and access, not just output or efficiency.
- A review can reveal that the original plan, design or allocation needs changing. That is learning, not necessarily failure.

The storm, greenhouse losses, reserve discussions and Drumroe delivery trial already provide story situations in which people notice consequences and adapt. They can become more deliberate over time. Do not claim that the village already operates a complete FRS simply because characters learn from experience.

## The systems form one continuous cycle

The guide's greenhouse example shows how the five systems connect:

1. **CDS** identifies a need and authorises a project.
2. **OAD** supplies or develops a design, considering materials and environmental impact.
3. **COS** organises people, tasks, tools and materials to make it happen.
4. **ITC** records contributions and calculates access values.
5. **FRS** monitors results, resource use, fairness and ecological impact.
6. Feedback returns to the relevant systems. The community may need a new decision, a revised design or a different way of organising the work.

This cycle is fundamental. The systems should not appear in the book as five unrelated gadgets or departments. A decision creates work; work produces records and outcomes; those outcomes generate feedback; feedback may lead to a changed decision or design.

For scenes, follow the consequences across the cycle. If a greenhouse plan is changed, show what prompted the change, who considered it, what practical work follows and how the group later knows whether the change helped.

## Nodes, networks and federation

The guide calls the basic unit a **node**. A node may be a neighbourhood, town, housing cooperative or another group organising itself using the framework. Each node operates the five systems at its own scale.

Nodes can connect through shared standards and a shared design library. They can share resources, labour and designs, and coordinate work that is beyond the capacity of one node. The guide describes this as federation: coordination grows across levels without a central headquarters or authority taking over.

**Story implications:**
- Ballywobble is a plausible small beginning, not a finished model for everyone else.
- Drumroe should not simply copy Ballywobble. It has its own needs, people, resources and limits.
- Communities can share a design, a method or a resource while adapting the arrangement locally.
- Wider coordination can emerge because groups use compatible standards and choose to cooperate, not because one village becomes the boss of the others.
- The network can make larger projects possible while leaving local decisions with the people affected by them.

The open question of whether and how the Shed scales remains open canon. Federation offers a direction to explore, not a predetermined ending.

## Transition: starting alongside the existing economy

The guide describes a voluntary, parallel transition rather than an overnight replacement of the existing economy. Small groups — such as mutual-aid networks, community gardens and tool libraries — begin by creating practical value. As groups adopt shared standards, they can connect and exchange resources. Networks may later federate to coordinate larger projects.

The guide stresses that nobody is forced to participate and that the process does not require taking political power before beginning.

This fits the existing story distinction between the Shed and **Ballywobble Odd Jobs**. The village has not abolished money or detached itself from the surrounding economy. It uses ordinary euros where it needs to deal with the outside world, while developing cooperative arrangements locally. Keep this tension real: outside suppliers, bills, regulation, scarce materials and people's existing livelihoods do not disappear.

## Technology: what it makes possible

The guide's software section describes tools to support all five systems. In plain terms, the software needs to support:
- proposals and participation in decisions;
- a searchable, version-controlled design library linked to production information;
- records of contributions and access, including credit calculations and use;
- coordination of work groups, schedules and resource flows;
- monitoring of environmental data, fairness measures and system performance, with alerts or recommendations;
- communication and coordination between federated communities.

The guide calls for open-source development and collaborative improvement of the software.

For the novel, technology should be treated as part of the practical infrastructure of the community — not as magic and not merely as a decorative tablet on a table. It can reduce the effort required to share information, find designs, coordinate work, record decisions and notice problems. It can also introduce questions about access, privacy, data quality, maintenance and who understands the tools.

Do not reduce the framework to the vague slogan that technology merely helps people make decisions. The guide assigns specific functions to the five systems and describes software supporting those functions. At the same time, do not invent technical capabilities that the source guide does not specify. When a scene depends on detailed software behaviour, consult the full technical paper or flag the gap for the author.

## How to bring this into the novel

The framework should become visible through use and consequence, not through explanatory speeches.

A scene can reveal a principle when:
- someone submits a proposal and another person records a concern that changes it (CDS);
- a villager finds an open design, adapts it to local materials and shares the improvement (OAD);
- the group asks how work and access are recorded, and how someone unable to contribute can still get what they need (ITC);
- a project stalls because the materials, skills or commitments are not coordinated, prompting a better arrangement (COS);
- a review reveals that a plan is causing waste, unfair workloads or an ecological problem, and the relevant process responds (FRS);
- Drumroe shares a useful method with Ballywobble but changes it to suit its own circumstances (nodes and federation).

Let different villagers understand different parts. Nobody needs to be an expert in all five systems. Ines may recognise relationships and ask questions; Mrs Kavanagh may test the records; Sam may notice what the plan requires in practice; Mabel may insist that the arrangement is workable; other villagers may contribute knowledge from their own experience. Keep them fallible and capable of changing their minds.

Technology can be ordinary and accessible where appropriate. The important question is not whether a scene contains an impressive app, but what information or coordination it makes possible, who can use it, and what happens when it is incomplete or wrong.

## Guardrails for continuity and tone

1. **Do not turn the novel into a manifesto or textbook.** Show the systems operating through scenes, dialogue, friction and practical outcomes.
2. **Do not understate the ambition.** This is not simply a story about a nice village sharing vegetables and tools. The villagers are beginning to explore a coherent, technology-enabled way of organising production, decisions, access and feedback.
3. **Do not pretend the full framework is already implemented.** The current Shed is an early and partial arrangement. Distinguish existing canon from future possibilities.
4. **Do not collapse the five systems into one app or one committee.** Keep their different responsibilities legible, and show how information and consequences move between them.
5. **Do not confuse coordination with command.** COS organises work and resources; the guide does not describe it as a new management hierarchy.
6. **Do not confuse ITC with money.** Preserve the Ledger's one-way valve and its established rules. Do not invent exchange with euros.
7. **Do not make technology infallible.** The guide describes monitoring, records, alerts and coordination; scenes can still explore incomplete information, maintenance, access and the need for review.
8. **Do not make every villager equally interested in the theory.** People can use a system because it solves a problem without adopting a complete ideology.
9. **Do not force scale.** Voluntary growth and federation are possibilities to explore. Whether the Shed scales remains unresolved.
10. **Keep the human pressures.** Time, skills, energy, privacy, livelihoods, ecological limits and disagreement must remain real.

## Quick reference

| Framework element | Core function | Existing story foothold |
|---|---|---|
| CDS | Proposals, degrees of support, objections, dialogue and recorded decisions | The Board and annoyance gauge — an early local practice, not the complete CDS |
| OAD | Open, shared designs that can be improved and reused | Ines's Notebook and the greenhouse plans — not yet a full design library |
| ITC | Contribution records and fair access values, not a currency | The Ledger — shares some principles, but is not automatically the full ITC model |
| COS | Cooperative organisation of people, tasks, materials and resources | The Rota, work groups and Odd Jobs coordination — early arrangements |
| FRS | Monitoring and feedback that prompt correction in the relevant system | Learning from the storm, reserves and Drumroe trial — currently mostly informal |
| Nodes and federation | Independent groups connect through shared standards and cooperation | Ballywobble and Drumroe; their differences must remain meaningful |
| Voluntary transition | Small groups build practical alternatives alongside the existing economy | The Shed and the euro-facing Odd Jobs co-op |
