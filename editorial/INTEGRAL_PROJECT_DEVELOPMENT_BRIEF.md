# INTEGRAL project development brief

> **Status: proposal, not canon.** This is a development document for discussion with the author. None of the project ideas, events, character decisions or technology features below are established story facts. Do not write chapter prose or update `STORY.md` from this brief without the author's approval.

## Purpose

The story is meant to make the five connected systems in Peter Joseph's INTEGRAL framework understandable through villagers actually using them. It should not retreat into a cosy account of people sharing vegetables and tools, nor become a lecture or manifesto. The systems should be visible because a real project puts pressure on the arrangements Ballywobble already has.

The village does not need to invent every mechanism from scratch. People can learn from existing knowledge, shared designs, other collectives and software that is already being developed. The challenge is to make capable infrastructure usable by ordinary people with limited time, money, skills and patience.

This brief proposes three connected development situations:

1. A substantial Ballywobble project that stretches the current arrangements.
2. A review that exposes a question of fairness, access or workload.
3. A neighbouring node adapting something and returning useful knowledge to the wider network.

They are story-development opportunities, not a prescribed three-chapter sequence. They may overlap, be combined, or be set aside.

## Guardrails from the existing story

- Ballywobble is an early and partial arrangement, not a fully implemented INTEGRAL node.
- The Board, Notebook, Ledger, Rota and informal reviews must not be silently relabelled as complete CDS, OAD, ITC, COS or FRS implementations.
- Keep the distinctions between the Ledger and euros, and between the Shed and the outside-facing Ballywobble Odd Jobs co-op.
- Drumroe has its own needs and people. It should adapt rather than copy Ballywobble.
- Do not make Ines the person who knows or explains everything. She can introduce a mechanism, ask a useful question or find a resource; other people must test, challenge and improve it.
- Characters have their own pressures and commitments. A good system does not make those disappear.
- Technology can be sophisticated underneath and straightforward to use. It should support the systems described in the framework, not replace them with a generic app or an all-knowing decision-maker.
- Keep the dry, warm, understated voice. Let readers understand through decisions, work, records, disagreements and consequences.

The five systems and their relationships should follow `editorial/INTEGRAL_IN_STORY.md` and the author's supplied plain-English guide. That editorial reference is a story-development interpretation of the supplied guide; it is not a substitute for checking the full technical paper where a detail matters.

## Situation A — A project bigger than the current arrangements

### Dramatic purpose

Give Ballywobble a project that cannot be handled comfortably by a few conversations, a paper list and the existing Rota. The pressure should arise naturally: several needs compete for the same people, space, materials, equipment, money or time. The group has to improve how it decides, designs and organises the work.

The project should be useful in its own right. It must not exist merely to demonstrate five acronyms.

### Candidate projects

These are alternatives, not decisions.

#### A1. Shared food storage and preservation

A practical response to seasonal gluts, waste and the limits of the current larder: some combination of shared storage, preserving, drying or processing.

**Why it has story potential**
- A good harvest is not automatically useful if there is nowhere suitable to store or process it.
- The project raises concrete questions about capacity, energy use, maintenance, food safety, access and who does recurring work.
- It naturally connects growing, storage, household needs and the outside suppliers or services that Odd Jobs may need to pay for.
- It is distinct from simply building another greenhouse.

**Systems that could become visible**
- **CDS:** compare different proposals and concerns; make the basis for the decision and the remaining disagreements visible.
- **OAD:** find and adapt an existing design; compare materials, energy demand, repairability, lifespan and local conditions; record revisions so another group can reuse the learning.
- **COS:** coordinate skills, tasks, materials, equipment, schedules and ongoing maintenance rather than just the initial build.
- **ITC:** record contributions and establish access values in a way that reflects the agreed model. Do not assume the Ledger already captures every relevant cost or that credits become money.
- **FRS:** compare expected and actual use, losses, energy/resource use, workload and access; send findings back to the parts of the arrangement that can change.

**Possible tensions**
- Some people want a simple low-cost solution; others worry about running costs or reliability.
- A design that works on paper may not suit the available building, power supply or maintenance skills.
- The build may be exciting, while cleaning, monitoring and upkeep are less attractive.
- A small number of people may end up carrying the recurring work.

**Unresolved questions**
- What exactly is stored or processed, and at what scale?
- What facilities already exist locally, and what would have to be bought or inspected externally?
- Is this one shared facility, or a smaller distributed arrangement?
- Who is willing to take responsibility for routine checks and maintenance?

#### A2. The second greenhouse, done differently

Use the existing open thread about a second greenhouse, but make the question more demanding than “shall we build another one?”

**Why it has story potential**
- It is already part of the ending and can show how the first build's experience changes the next decision.
- The group now has evidence about storm damage, reserves, design compromises, maintenance and demand.
- It can reveal whether the village can reuse and improve a design rather than simply repeat a successful project.

**Systems that could become visible**
- **CDS:** distinguish support for the idea from agreement about location, scale, cost, water, resilience and responsibility.
- **OAD:** compare the original design with revisions and external designs; preserve why changes were made.
- **COS:** test whether labour, skills, materials and maintenance capacity exist alongside the enthusiasm to build.
- **ITC:** make contribution and access arrangements clear without turning credits into a price or a barrier to essentials.
- **FRS:** use evidence from the first greenhouse to revise the proposal and later check whether the changes worked.

**Possible tensions**
- Some villagers may see the second greenhouse as obvious progress; others may ask whether the first one is being maintained properly.
- The best design may be more expensive initially but easier to repair or more resilient.
- People who did the first build may not want to repeat the work.

**Risk**
This could feel like a repeat of Chapter Four unless the central problem is genuinely new: for example, competing uses of land and water, maintenance capacity, or how the first greenhouse's performance data changes the design.

#### A3. A shared repair and equipment facility

A coordinated place or service for maintaining, repairing and sharing equipment that is currently scattered across sheds, homes and local businesses.

**Why it has story potential**
- It connects naturally to the existing tool-lending arrangement and O'Driscoll's shop without making him a member.
- Repair work creates visible dependencies: tools, parts, skills, safe storage, booking, maintenance and outside purchases.
- It could make the relationship between local knowledge, shared designs and actual production tangible.

**Systems that could become visible**
- **CDS:** decide what the facility is for, what it will not take on and how competing priorities are resolved.
- **OAD:** shared repair instructions, parts information and improved designs.
- **COS:** match jobs with skills, equipment, parts and available time; manage queues and maintenance.
- **ITC:** record contributions and access consistently with the agreed rules.
- **FRS:** track repair success, repeat failures, waiting times, equipment downtime and unmet needs.

**Possible tensions**
- A shared resource can create expectations that someone will always be available.
- Some repairs need specialist parts, certification or paid expertise.
- O'Driscoll may benefit from the demand but remain wary of being treated as an unpaid extension of the Shed.

### Initial editorial recommendation

**A1, shared food storage and preservation, is the strongest fresh direction to explore first.** It grows out of the existing food story but moves beyond growing and sharing. It creates a credible reason to connect design, resource planning, contribution records and feedback. It also offers ordinary, tangible stakes: food that is available at the wrong time, equipment that costs money to run, and work that still needs doing after the opening day.

That is a recommendation for the next discussion, not a canon decision. A2 has the advantage of an existing open thread; A3 has the advantage of bringing repair, skills and local businesses into sharper focus.

## Situation B — The review finds something unfair or unsustainable

### Dramatic purpose

A project can appear successful in totals while concealing who is doing the work, who can access the result, what is being wasted or whose needs are not being met. A review should reveal a mismatch between the intended arrangement and lived experience.

This is not a morality play about someone cheating. The issue should emerge from incomplete information, a design assumption, an unintended incentive or different circumstances.

### Candidate pressure points

- **Workload concentration:** the records show that a small number of people are repeatedly doing essential or unglamorous tasks. Others may be willing but unavailable at the times the work is needed.
- **Access and contribution:** a household needs something but cannot contribute in the usual way or at the expected time. The group has to examine whether its access rules and records reflect the principles it says it follows.
- **A misleading value:** an access value or contribution record no longer reflects the actual effort, resource use or conditions. A change in production efficiency or an overlooked cost exposes the problem.
- **A quiet bottleneck:** the project is technically working, but one person, tool, supplier or approval step is holding everything up.

### Systems that could become visible

- **FRS:** the review brings together actual outcomes, not just a success story. It identifies what needs attention and routes feedback to the relevant system.
- **ITC:** the group examines what is being recorded and how access values are determined. Keep the distinction between contribution records, access values and money precise.
- **CDS:** people affected by the problem can raise concerns and influence a revision. The group records the reasons and the decision rather than relying on whoever speaks loudest.
- **COS:** tasks, responsibilities, capacity and resources are adjusted in response. A rota should not quietly turn a volunteer into a permanent obligation.

### Character possibilities

- **Mrs Kavanagh** notices that the records answer one question but not the one people are now asking.
- **Sam** identifies the practical work or maintenance that the plan has failed to count.
- **Mabel** keeps the discussion grounded in what people can realistically manage.
- **Niamh** is alert to the point where a voluntary contribution becomes an assumed duty.
- **Ines** can help trace the gap, but should also be open to the possibility that the original model or her own assumptions caused it.

No character needs to deliver a speech about fairness. Let the evidence, the people affected and the revised arrangement carry the meaning.

### Unresolved questions

- Which pressure point is most believable after the project in Situation A?
- Who notices it first, and who is affected but reluctant to speak?
- What does the current information fail to show?
- What can be changed without pretending that every conflict can be solved?

## Situation C — Another node adapts and shares what it learns

### Dramatic purpose

Show that the work is no longer only a local Ballywobble arrangement. Another group uses shared knowledge, changes it to suit its own conditions and feeds its experience back. The important development is reciprocal learning, not expansion under Ballywobble's direction.

Drumroe is the natural starting point because it already has its own people, trial and distribution problem. It must remain distinct: its needs are not Ballywobble's needs.

### Possible development

A Drumroe project encounters a problem that Ballywobble has already met in a different form. The people there look through shared designs or recorded experience, adapt one element, and report what happened. Ballywobble may discover that Drumroe's change is useful to them too.

The shared resource could include:
- a searchable collection of designs, instructions and versions;
- the reasons for design choices, including known limitations;
- records of materials, energy, labour, repair and maintenance;
- feedback from real use, including failed attempts;
- a way for separate groups to share improvements without surrendering local decision-making.

The exact software implementation is open. Do not invent a feature merely because it would be convenient for the plot; agree what the author's developing system can plausibly support.

### Systems that could become visible

- **OAD:** a design is found, adapted, versioned and returned with evidence about what changed and why.
- **FRS:** experience from use is captured and reaches the people or groups able to improve the design or process.
- **COS:** the local group coordinates its own people, skills, materials and work to implement the adaptation.
- **CDS:** the receiving group decides what is appropriate locally; shared knowledge informs its decision but does not make it for them.
- **ITC:** contribution and access records remain intelligible across the participating arrangements, without assuming that one village's local credit rules automatically transfer to another.

### Technology and federation

This is an opportunity to make the technology more visible as shared infrastructure. It should not be a magic central platform that commands the villages. Show the useful connective work: locating a design, seeing its version and provenance, understanding its resource requirements, recording a local adaptation, and making the resulting feedback available to others.

Keep the distinction between:
- a shared design or standard that helps groups cooperate;
- a local decision made by the people who will live with it;
- a network of independent nodes;
- a central authority directing those nodes.

The story can show a small, imperfect instance of inter-node cooperation. It should not imply that a complete federation already exists.

### Possible tensions

- The design that worked in Ballywobble may fail in Drumroe because the conditions differ.
- A group may want the benefit of shared knowledge without having time to document its own changes.
- A standard can make cooperation easier but may not fit every local circumstance.
- A successful adaptation may reveal a flaw in the original design, which requires Ballywobble to revise its own practice.

### Unresolved questions

- Is Drumroe the first group to contribute a reusable adaptation, or is another nearby group involved?
- What specific design or process is shared?
- What information is genuinely useful to the receiving group?
- How does the story show reciprocal benefit without making the villages compete or turning one into the teacher?

## Making the software present without making it the story

The technology should become more than a collection of unrelated conveniences, but it should be encountered through ordinary tasks rather than a product demonstration.

A useful progression might be:

1. **Find:** someone locates a relevant proposal, design, record or previous review.
2. **Understand:** they can see its context, version, assumptions and limitations.
3. **Participate:** people can contribute concerns, support, evidence or availability without all needing to be in the same room at the same time.
4. **Coordinate:** agreed work can be connected to people, skills, materials, tools and schedules.
5. **Record:** contributions, resource use and access values are recorded according to the relevant system.
6. **Review:** actual outcomes are compared with expectations; issues are routed back to the people or process able to respond.
7. **Share:** useful learning can be made available to other nodes, which can adopt or adapt it.

This is a narrative capability map, not a claim that one existing application already implements every feature. The five systems remain distinct and connected. The user experience can be simple while the underlying infrastructure is substantial.

A scene should normally show only the part of the technology that matters to the immediate problem. Let the reader infer the larger system from repeated use and connections between scenes.

## Character ownership: avoid making Ines the whole system

| Character | Natural contribution | Guard against |
|---|---|---|
| Ines | Finds patterns, asks questions, locates designs and helps connect information | Becoming the lecturer or the person with every answer |
| Mabel | Keeps decisions grounded in actual village circumstances | Becoming an all-purpose wise voice |
| Mrs Kavanagh | Tests records, definitions, accountability and consistency | Reducing her to a procedural obstacle |
| Sam | Sees production, repair, maintenance and practical capacity | Making him dismissive of every abstract idea |
| Hob | Notices whether a proposed arrangement will work in practice | Turning his quiet judgement into speeches |
| Gerard | Thinks about reserves, equipment and what might go wrong | Making suspicion his only mode |
| O'Driscoll | Brings outside expertise, parts, supply and a shopkeeper's perspective | Making him a covert member of the Shed |
| Andrew | Pushes for named responsibility and reliable coordination | Making his preference for structure simply wrong |
| Mary O'Shea | Protects local context and the people a process is meant to serve | Making Drumroe a copy of Ballywobble |
| Niamh | Keeps volunteer boundaries and real capacity visible | Treating her caution as obstruction |

Other characters can own ideas too. A system becomes credible when no single person is its inventor, operator and advocate.

## Suggested development sequence

This is a working order for exploration, not a fixed plot outline.

1. **Choose Situation A.** Decide which project creates the richest new practical problem without repeating an existing chapter.
2. **Write a one-page scene brief for Situation A.** Specify the event, triggering problem, people involved, relevant systems, what the reader learns, consequences and what remains unresolved.
3. **Derive Situation B from the project.** The fairness or workload issue should arise from the actual design and operation, not be bolted on as a separate lesson.
4. **Connect Situation C to a real artefact or learning.** Another node should have a concrete reason to find, adapt or contribute something.
5. **Review the connected cycle.** Check that the story shows how a decision, design, coordinated work, contribution/resource records and feedback affect one another.
6. **Only then decide where scenes belong.** Existing open threads may be developed; new chapters are not assumed.
