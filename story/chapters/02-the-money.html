Chapter Two

The Money

The first person to ask where the money was coming from was Mrs Kavanagh.

This was not because she was particularly interested in money. It was because she was particularly interested in knowing where it had gone.

Mrs Kavanagh was a retired accountant who still kept her household accounts in a ledger, used a calculator with a paper roll and regarded the phrase "roughly speaking" as a personal attack. Her house smelled of lavender polish and old paper. She wore a red cardigan in all weathers and kept a red pen in its pocket, and it was generally understood in Ballywobble that if she took the pen out in your presence, you had already been found wanting.

She had been married to a man who had once bought a second-hand caravan without checking whether it had wheels. She had learned early that enthusiasm is no substitute for arithmetic.

For three weeks she had watched the Shed from her front window. Strictly speaking she couldn't see the Shed; she could see a hawthorn, a bend in the lane and, if she stood a little to the left of the good armchair, the gap through which people went to Sam Watts's yard. But she could count, and she had counted. Eleven people on the Tuesday. A trailer of timber going in. Nothing coming out, except people, who came out looking pleased with themselves, which she had always regarded as a bad sign in a financial context.

Nobody had mentioned money. That was what she couldn't get past. In three weeks of eleven people on a Tuesday, nobody had mentioned it once; and in her experience, when nobody mentions money, somebody is already paying for something and hasn't noticed.

She came down the lane on the Thursday with a cardboard folder held flat against her chest, like a woman carrying a tray through a crowd.

The yard was Sam's before it was anybody else's: a square of cracked concrete behind the garage, a lean-to roof, a corrugated shed with a door that wouldn't close and a view of the river if you stood on a bucket. Since the lettuce it had acquired a trestle table, two kettles and a noticeboard with nothing on it.

It was drizzling, in the way West Cork drizzles when it has no particular plans. Mabel Brennan sat on an upturned crate with her coat zipped to the chin, doing what she called not chairing. Ines sat beside her with the Notebook open on her knee and a feed bag held over it like a small tent. Hob stood at the trestle with the carburettor of a lawnmower laid out in pieces on a sheet of newspaper, in the manner of a man conducting a very slow post-mortem. Sam was under the lean-to, looking for something. And at the gate, in a van with the engine running, Gerard O'Callaghan was just passing, as he had been just passing for eleven days.

"Good afternoon," said Mrs Kavanagh.

"It isn't a meeting," said Mabel.

"I didn't say it was."

"You've a folder."

"I always have a folder."

Mabel looked at the folder for some time. Sam, without being asked, produced an upturned bucket and wiped it with his sleeve, which made it worse. Mrs Kavanagh sat on it as though it were a chair.

"I have a question," she said.

"Go on," said Mabel, in the voice of a woman who would rather not.

"Where is the money coming from?"

Nobody answered. Hob turned a screw a quarter turn and turned it back.

"What money?" said Sam.

"Exactly."

She opened the folder. Inside was a single sheet, ruled in columns with an actual ruler, in handwriting so even it might have been printed. She read from it without looking up.

"Timber. Nil."

"That's mine," said Sam.

"Which is why it says nil. You haven't charged for it."

"It was lying about."

"Everything's lying about until somebody needs it, Sam. That's what makes it stock." She moved down the page. "Seeds. Compost. Fixings. A licence for the strip by the river, which belongs to the council, and the council has not, to my knowledge, ever accepted thanks. Glass. Electricity, if anyone's thinking of a heater, and I've seen the drawing, Ines, and you're thinking of a heater."

"A small one," said Ines.

"It's the electricity company. They don't do small."

Nobody had ever established a price at which the electricity company would accept gratitude. Mrs Kavanagh had a suspicion that nobody had ever asked.

"And tea," she said. "Forty-one cups since Monday. I asked Sam."

"I told her," said Sam. "I didn't think she'd write it down."

"I bring the tea," said Mabel.

"I know. Three boxes. I've been keeping that too."

"You've been counting my tea."

"Someone had to."

Mabel said nothing, which in Mabel was a considerable remark.

At the gate the engine of the van went off. This was as close as Gerard came to committing to anything. He wound the window down two inches.

"I might have a few windows," he said.

"How many?" said Mabel.

"I'm not saying."

"Gerard."

"I'm not saying I haven't."

Mrs Kavanagh took out the red pen. Gerard watched it come out of the pocket and the window went up again by an inch. She wrote Windows (unconfirmed) at the bottom of the page, and underlined unconfirmed twice.

"May I see the Notebook?" she said.

Ines handed it over. Mrs Kavanagh turned the pages slowly, the way some people handle a stranger's baby. Ines was twenty, with dark wavy hair she had tied back against the damp, and a way of looking at you as if she had already seen where this was going and was too polite to say. It was not an unfriendly look. It did, however, have the effect of making people feel they had been steered somewhere gently and had said yes before anyone asked the question. Mrs Kavanagh was not susceptible to it, as a rule. She noticed that she was looking at the Notebook rather harder than she needed to.

"Materials," she read. "Energy. Labour. Environmental cost."

"Those are the four things I track."

"Where's the fifth?"

"There isn't a fifth."

"There is, Ines. It's the one that goes in the bank."

Ines considered this. She did not defend the Notebook, which Mrs Kavanagh had half expected; she asked a question instead.

"What would it tell us? A column for euros."

"How much we owe."

"We don't owe anything."

"Not yet. That's what I'm trying to preserve." She tapped the page. "And the environmental cost. How is that measured?"

"It's an estimate. How much damage a thing does."

"In euros?"

"No."

"Then it's not a cost, dear. It's a worry." She said it kindly, and Ines, to her credit, wrote it down.

"We could trade for the glass," said Ines. "People trade. Sam traded the gate."

"I didn't trade the gate," said Sam. "I fixed a gate."

"And what did you get?"

Sam's attention drifted to the roof of the lean-to.

"Sam."

"A cake."

"What kind?"

"Lemon."

"And the hinges? What did the hinges cost?"

"Thirty-odd."

"Then you paid thirty euro for a lemon cake."

"It was a good cake."

"My wife says the same thing," he added, to nobody, a moment later.

"She's right," said Mrs Kavanagh. "And the glazier in Skibbereen will not want a gate, however well hung."

"Has anyone asked him?" said Ines.

There was a pause. It was a short pause, and the only evidence that it had occurred was that Mrs Kavanagh had stopped writing.

"No," she said.

"Then we don't know."

"I know."

"How?"

"I have met glaziers."

Ines nodded, and wrote something in the margin that she did not show anyone.

"Right," said Mabel, and stood up, which was a thing you did not do if you were not chairing. She appeared not to notice this. "Money. Does anyone object to there being some?"

Nobody objected. Nobody looked especially keen, either.

"Not much," said Mrs Kavanagh. "But some. And it has to be kept separate from what we're doing here."

She explained that they could register a small cooperative, which would provide ordinary services to local businesses. They could fix the pub's cellar, repair shelving at the vet, maintain the shop's refrigeration equipment and take whatever other jobs people were happy to pay for. The money would go into an ordinary bank account. The cooperative would pay its taxes, settle its invoices and use what was left to buy the things the village couldn't provide itself.

"I'm not joining anything," said Sam.

"You won't be joining. You'll be a member."

"What's the difference?"

"There's a form."

"I will in my hat."

"And you'll be paid."

"I don't want to be paid."

"That," said Mrs Kavanagh, "is the other thing I wanted to discuss."

She turned the folder over and drew on the back of it: a rectangle, a door in the rectangle and an arrow through the door, pointing in.

"This is us. Money comes in at this door."

"From the pub," said Sam.

"From the pub, from the vet, from anyone willing to pay you for something you're already good at. It goes into an account. And it goes out only to buy what we can't make ourselves." She drew a second arrow, small, leaving by the bottom. "That's the one way it leaves."

"And the ticks?" said Ines. She had been keeping them in the margin of the Notebook since the lettuce: one tick for an hour of somebody's help, a name beside it. Mabel called it the book of grievances.

"The ticks stay inside." She drew a circle within the rectangle, well away from the door. "What we produce or repair here, we count here. Nothing that came through the door goes into the circle. Nothing in the circle goes out through it. Euros buy from outside. Credits are for in here. The two never swap."

She looked at the diagram.

"It's a one-way valve."

"Non-return valve," said Hob, without looking up from the carburettor.

"In my house it's a one-way valve."

"Fair enough."

Hob said very little and fixed most things, and the yard had grown used to the fact that his remarks tended to arrive from underneath something. This was the first time he had spoken that afternoon. He spoke again.

"Why?"

"Because the moment you can exchange one for the other, you've made a currency. And the moment you've made a currency, somebody will eventually find a way to collect it."

Hob said nothing. He picked up a small spring, looked at it and put it down in a different place on the newspaper. Mabel, who was watching him, said nothing either.

"And if somebody had a lot of credits," said Ines, "and their boiler went?"

"Then they'd buy a boiler."

"With the credits?"

"With euros, like everyone else in the country."

"So the credits can't help."

"The boiler is euros. The man who fits it, if he's Sam, is credits."

"I'm not fitting anyone's boiler," said Sam.

"Hypothetically."

"Hypothetically I'm not fitting it either."

Ines wrote that down as well. Then she crossed it out and wrote something shorter.

Sam looked at the diagram for a long time. It was a very simple diagram. It did not appear to improve with attention.

"So we're a business?"

"Partly."

"And a charity?"

"No."

"Then what are we?"

Mrs Kavanagh put down her pen.

"At the moment, Robert, we're a group of people who need a greenhouse and haven't yet worked out what to call ourselves."

It took them until the following Wednesday to work out what to call themselves, and they did it at Mrs Kavanagh's kitchen table, because the form had to be filled in somewhere with a flat surface and a decent light, and she would not have it done on a trestle.

The form was four pages long. It asked for the name of the society, its registered address, its objects and a minimum number of members, which Mrs Kavanagh counted twice, and then once more, because the number was three more than the number of people who would admit to being members.

"We could be the Shed," said Mabel.

"A society cannot be named after its premises."

"It's not even our premises. It's Sam's."

"Then it's even less suitable."

"Ballywobble Collective," said Ines.

"No," said Mrs Kavanagh.

"God, no," said Mabel.

"It's only a word."

"It's a word," said Mrs Kavanagh, "that will be read by somebody who has never met any of us, in a room we will never see, and it will not be read generously. We'll not be giving anyone ideas." She wrote Collective on the back of an envelope and struck it through, and then, after a moment, struck through Commune, which nobody had suggested.

"Community Enterprise," she offered.

Nobody said anything. The kitchen clock, which Mrs Kavanagh kept five minutes fast on principle, ticked into the silence.

"Odd jobs," said Sam.

"That is a description. It is not a name."

"It's what we do."

"Then it's accurate," said Hob.

Mrs Kavanagh looked at the form. She looked at the envelope. She looked at the word Objects, in bold, followed by four lines for the cooperative to explain what it was for, and found that she could not think of a single sentence that would improve on it.

"Ballywobble Odd Jobs," she said, and wrote it in capitals.

Mabel, who had been looking at the word Objects as well, said it was a funny thing to call a purpose. Nobody disagreed with her. Mrs Kavanagh filled in the four lines in a hand so small and so exact that the Registrar, six weeks later, was reported to have read it twice.

The first job was the pub.

The cellar cooling had been failing since the spring, and the publican had been telling people so for most of that time, in the tone of a man describing a long illness. Hob looked at it for four minutes. Sam looked at it for ten. They disagreed about the cause, which turned out to be a seized pump, and Hob was right, and the two of them spent most of an afternoon in the damp with a torch held in somebody's teeth.

When it was done the publican said, "Put it on my tab," and Sam, who would have said "grand" and gone home, said "grand."

"It's not a tab, it's an invoice," said Mrs Kavanagh, who had come down with a pad and was standing at the top of the cellar steps like a customs officer.

"Since when?" said the publican.

"Since Wednesday week. Sam. What do we charge?"

"Fifty?"

"For two men and an afternoon?"

"Sixty."

She wrote 140 + parts on the pad and tore it off, and Sam went the colour of the cellar wall. The publican read the invoice and looked at the pump and looked at Sam.

"Is that all?"

He paid it before he had finished the question. He paid it in notes, from a drawer, and then asked for a receipt, because Mrs Kavanagh was holding a receipt book and he had the look of a man who had accidentally joined something. Sam stood in the doorway of the cellar holding the money. The price had been fine all along. That was the part he found hardest to forgive.

Mrs Kavanagh took the notes without ceremony, counted them into an envelope and wrote PAID across the invoice in red. She did it the way other people cross themselves.

Ines rang the glazier in Skibbereen that evening. She had meant to do it on her own, and then found she'd have been embarrassed to be seen not doing it, and rang him from the Prassa with the door of the office closed.

He was very kind about it. He listened to the whole proposal, which took some time, and said it sounded like a lovely idea, and asked who would be paying.

She told Mrs Kavanagh the next morning, on the doorstep, with her hair still damp from the rain.

"He wants a bank transfer."

"I know."

"I'd thought we could do most of it with credits," said Ines. "I think I'd that wrong."

Mrs Kavanagh did not say she had told her so. She said, "Thank you for ringing," and wrote it in the folder, and Ines understood that this was the greater courtesy.

The first thing Ballywobble Odd Jobs bought with its money was tea.

The glass would have to wait for the next job, and the one after that. The motion was proposed by Mabel and carried without discussion; Mrs Kavanagh abstained, and recorded her abstention. She placed the receipt in the folder beside the invoice, under a single line in red. Six euro forty. The box sat in the cupboard at the back of the yard for two days before anyone touched it, as if it might go off.

By the end of the first month, Sam's yard had a new gutter, a patched roof, a door that closed properly and a bench that somebody had sanded and oiled without being asked. Sam complained about all of it. He also started leaving the shed unlocked.

Mr Pennyfeather, when he eventually inspected the bank account two years later, found it entirely conventional.

He found this the most suspicious thing of all.
