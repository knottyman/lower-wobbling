Chapter Three

People Who Weren't Convinced

Not everyone was delighted.

Mr McKenna, the shopkeeper, was initially convinced the Shed was going to destroy his business.

He had worked in the shop since he was sixteen. His father had owned it before him, and his father's name was still over the door, in letters that had faded to the colour of weak tea. He knew which customers preferred brown bread, which ones would ask for credit and which ones would return a tin of beans because the label was slightly crooked. Under the till he kept a thick school copybook in which the credit was written down in pencil: who owed what, since when, and whether it was worth mentioning. Nobody had ever called it a ledger, least of all him. It was the book.

He was not a bad man. He was a man with a mortgage, a wife who had recently retired and a business that had barely survived the previous three winters.

Free lettuce did not sound particularly attractive.

The lettuces had begun to appear at the end of the lane in a wooden crate, with a sign in Mabel's handwriting. The sign said LETTUCE. TAKE ONE. DON'T BE GREEDY. It was the only piece of marketing the Shed had ever done, and it was entirely successful, which Mr McKenna found harder to bear than failure would have been.

On the Tuesday a woman came in for a loaf and a pint of milk and set a lettuce on the counter beside them.

"Is that one of theirs?" said Mr McKenna.

"It's a lettuce."

"It's a free lettuce."

"It's a good lettuce." She paid for the bread and the milk, counted her change and put the lettuce in her bag. "I'd have bought yours, only yours is at its best on a Monday."

His own lettuce came on a lorry on Mondays. She was not wrong. This did not help.

For a month he told anyone who would listen that free food was the thin end of a very large wedge. He told the postman, the wholesaler's rep and a man who had only come in to ask directions. His wife, who had spent forty years listening to him and had recently stopped having to, asked him which wedge.

"That's the point of a wedge," said Mr McKenna. "You don't see the rest of it."

Then his chiller broke.

It broke on the last warm Thursday of the year, at about four in the afternoon, with a noise like a man clearing his throat in a very large room. By five the milk had stopped being cold. By six he had rung the commercial repair company in Cork, who were very sympathetic and could send somebody on Tuesday week, subject to a call-out fee that he asked them to repeat.

"Ring the lettuce people," said his wife.

"I will not."

"Sam Watts is a mechanic. He fixed Nora Deasy's fridge."

"For a cake."

"Well, the fridge works."

He held out until twenty to seven, by which time there were eleven litres of milk, four trays of yoghurt and the best part of a ham between him and the bin. Then he rang, in a voice that suggested the call was being made under duress, and said that if Sam happened to be passing he might call in. He did not say please. He had prepared a speech for the other end of the line, about not wanting charity, and found that he had no way of using it.

Sam came over with a toolbox and a flask and stood looking at the chiller for some time, in the way mechanics have, as if it might volunteer something. Then he took the back off.

"It's the compressor," he said.

"Is that bad?"

"It's not good. I've one at home that'll fit. Give me the afternoon."

It took the afternoon, and most of the evening. Mr McKenna hovered. He offered tea, which was accepted, and a packet of biscuits, which was accepted more quickly. At nine o'clock the machine began to hum, and a little after that the milk went back in.

"What do I owe you?" said Mr McKenna, finally, with his hand already on the till.

"Ask Mrs Kavanagh."

"Who?"

"She does the invoices."

The invoice came by hand on the Saturday, in a plain envelope, itemised on a single sheet. A compressor, at cost. Labour, at a rate that was visibly below the going one. Payment within thirty days. There was nothing written in red.

He read it standing at the till, and then read it again, because he had come in with the speech ready to refuse a favour and had been handed a bill instead. A favour he could have turned down. He could not turn down an invoice. An invoice meant he had bought something, and a man who has bought something does not owe anybody anything.

It came to eighty euro less than the man from Cork had quoted before he'd seen the machine.

He paid on the Monday, in cash, in an envelope with Thank you written on the front, in a hand that had plainly taken several attempts. The chiller worked better than it had in years.

"You'd be welcome," said Sam, a week later, collecting the empty envelope, "if you wanted to come down some Saturday."

"I'm not joining anything."

"Nobody's asking you to."

"Good."

There was a pause, during which Mr McKenna rearranged a display of tinned peaches that had not needed rearranging.

"I might put a few of their lettuces by the till," he said. "Just the ones that are going spare."

By autumn, he had a shelf beside the register marked LOCAL. His wife had made the sign. He described it to the wholesaler's rep as a trial. It was still a trial the following spring.

Mr O'Driscoll, however, was not persuaded.

He owned the hardware shop and had sold ladders, drills, spades and wheelbarrows to the village for twenty-six years. His shop smelled of sawdust, linseed oil and old iron. It was a place where you could buy a single nail or a bag of cement, and where O'Driscoll would tell you exactly how to use both, whether you asked or not.

The Shed had started lending those very things out.

It happened quietly. A tool library, Ines called it, though nobody else did, and Hob kept it in the shed at the back of Sam's yard on a pegboard he'd drawn the outlines on in marker, so you could see what was missing. Ladders, drills, a wheelbarrow with a wobble, three spades and a hedge trimmer of which everyone was a little afraid. They went out on a Saturday with a name on a card and they came back, more or less, on the Monday.

He was losing business. He did the books on a Sunday evening, with the shop shut and the lights off at the back, and the figure for ladders was three since March. It had been eleven in the same stretch the year before.

On the Thursday a man who had bought a wheelbarrow from him every second spring for as long as he could remember came in for a box of screws and said, pleasantly, that he'd borrowed one off the Shed this time and it was grand. He said it the way you'd mention the weather. O'Driscoll sold him the screws.

He was also, although he would never admit it, frightened.

His father had built the shop from nothing. His father had spent years paying off the mortgage, negotiating with suppliers and keeping the doors open through recessions and bad winters, and had passed the whole thing on to him, along with a very particular way of coiling rope. Now a group of people with a shed and an exercise book seemed to be telling the village that it didn't need him.

The village was changing, as villages do. There were new faces at the school gate and new names on the bins. He did not especially mind that. What he minded was that it had all happened without anyone calling into the shop to ask.

One evening he met Sam outside the pub. The air was cold, and carried the smell of rain and stale beer. Sam had come straight from a job and was holding a rag; O'Driscoll was holding his car keys and had been for some minutes.

"You're giving things away," he said.

"We're lending things."

"Same difference."

"No, it isn't."

"People need to buy things, Sam. That's how businesses work."

Sam wiped his hands on the rag.

"People need ladders. They don't necessarily need to own a ladder."

"Well, I sell ladders."

"I know."

"Three since March. Eleven last year."

"A ladder lasts. That's the point of a ladder."

"It's a funny sort of point for a man who's never had to sell one." O'Driscoll turned the keys over in his hand. "You're not trying to put me out of business. I know that."

"We're not."

"Nobody ever is," said O'Driscoll. "It just happens, and everyone's very sorry."

Sam opened his mouth and found he had nothing in it. Somewhere behind them, in the pub, a glass was put down and somebody laughed.

"And what happens when your lot break one?" said O'Driscoll.

"We fix it."

"And what happens when you can't?"

Sam paused.

"Then we buy one from you."

O'Driscoll looked at him.

"Very clever."

There was no answer to that.

He went home angry, and Sam went home slowly, which was worse.

"Well?" said his wife, who was at the kitchen table with a calculator and a stack of somebody else's receipts. She kept the books for a firm in the town, and had done for fourteen years, and could add up a column of figures in the time it took Sam to find his glasses.

"Eleven last year," said Sam. "Three since March."

"That's a lot of ladders."

"It's the same number of people."

"Did you tell him that?"

"He'd have hit me."

"He's not that sort."

"Everyone's that sort about a ladder."

She turned a page. "Did he have a point?"

Sam considered the question for longer than he'd have liked.

"About three ladders' worth," he said.

The next morning Mabel found a letter in the Shed's postbox, which was a biscuit tin screwed to the gate.

It was from a supermarket chain, and it was on very good paper. She took it down the lane and into the yard, where Ines was untangling a hose and Mrs Kavanagh was standing in the doorway of the shed with a mug, supervising nothing in particular.

"It's addressed to the Shed," said Mabel. "It says The Occupier."

"Which of us is the occupier?" said Ines.

"Sam, technically. He owns the yard." Mabel read the first page in the voice she used for the small print on an insurance policy. "It has come to our attention that the term Market Garden has been used in an online notice in connection with the supply of fresh produce in your area. Our client holds registered rights in a number of related marks, and is concerned that such use may cause confusion among consumers."

She looked up.

"Our client is a supermarket."

"Where was the notice?" said Ines.

"It was one line on the parish page. It said there'd be a market garden by the river. Eventually."

"Who wrote it?"

Nobody would admit to having written it. Mrs Kavanagh regarded this as an accounting failure in its own right.

"Which words, exactly?" said Ines.

"Market Garden."

"Both of them?"

"It says both."

"Together, or separately?"

"Does it matter?"

"It might," said Ines, and she took the letter out of Mabel's hand and began to read it again from the top, with a faint frown, as though somebody had just started to explain something very slowly and she was waiting for it to turn into sense.

Mrs Kavanagh took it from her at the second page.

She read it twice.

Then she took out her red pen.

She was looking forward to this.
