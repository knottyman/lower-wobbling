Chapter Four

The Greenhouse

The letter from the supermarket chain had been, Mrs Kavanagh decided, a masterpiece of corporate hedging. It expressed "concern" about the use of the phrase Market Garden in an online notice, cited "brand alignment" and "territorial clarity", and suggested a "without prejudice conversation" at their earliest convenience.

She read it twice, then took out her red pen.

Her reply was three sentences long. It explained that the Shed had no interest in becoming a supermarket, that the words Market Garden had been chosen because they described a garden where things were sold at a market, and that if the chain wished to discuss territorial clarity they were welcome to visit on a Thursday, when the tea was hot and the opinions were not for sale.

She did not receive a reply. The notice remained unchanged.

Mabel pinned the original letter to the noticeboard in Sam's yard, which until then had nothing on it. Underneath, in Mrs Kavanagh's hand, was a single word: ANSWERED.

By the time the Shed had thirty-one members, a small account at an ordinary bank, a larder in the old chapel and a growing collection of borrowed tools, Ines had noticed something.

The village was still losing half its winter greens to frost.

She noticed it on a Tuesday in November, at ten past eight in the morning, standing beside Mabel in the garden behind Mabel's house. Mabel was looking at a row of winter lettuce that had been, on Monday, a row of winter lettuce, and was now a row of something else. The leaves had gone the colour and texture of green glass. When Mabel touched one it simply gave up.

She had put fleece over them. She had put the fleece on twice.

Ines stood with her hands in her sleeves and said nothing, in a way that Mabel could feel on the side of her face.

"Don't," said Mabel.

"I haven't said anything."

"You were going to say greenhouse."

"I was going to ask what time it came."

"About four. It always comes about four."

"And the fleece? Twice?"

"Twice."

"Would a third time have done it?"

Mabel considered the lettuce. "No."

"What would?"

Mabel turned and looked at her.

"You've the look," she said.

"What look?"

"The one that makes a person agree to things."

"I only asked what would."

"A roof," said Mabel, after a time. "Glass. Something over them."

Ines opened her mouth.

"Don't say it."

"We'd need to talk about it properly," said Ines. "All of us. Where it goes. What it costs. Who'd have to give up what."

"I'm not having a meeting."

"Then don't call it one."

"What would I call it?"

"A gathering."

"That's a meeting in a cardigan."

Ines waited. She was good at it. After some time Mabel stirred the dead lettuce with the toe of her boot and said that it could be Thursday, at eight, and that people were to bring their own tea.

The notice went up in McKenna's shop that afternoon. Gathering at The Prassa. Thursday. 8pm. Bring your own tea. Mr McKenna pinned it beside the shelf marked LOCAL, without being asked, and without a word, which was the most generous thing he had ever done in connection with the Shed.

Mabel Brennan hated meetings. She had spent seventy-one years avoiding them. She had a deep and abiding suspicion of anyone who said "we should have a chat about this", because it usually meant two hours in a cold hall listening to someone who liked the sound of their own voice. She stood in the doorway of the shop and read the notice twice to make sure the word "meeting" was nowhere in it.

They met in the back room of The Prassa. It was smaller than a barn, and warmer, and there was tea, and Ines's mother had put out a plate of sandwiches on the grounds that nobody makes good decisions on an empty stomach.

Ines brought a diagram. It was a good diagram. It showed a greenhouse with raised beds, a rainwater collection system and a small potting area. She had even drawn little arrows showing where the sun would come from.

She also brought a screen. It was an old tablet, propped up against the sugar bowl.

Sam arrived first. He looked at the tablet suspiciously.

"What's that for?"

"It's for the Board," said Ines.

"What board?"

"The digital board. For proposals and objections."

Sam looked at the tablet. He looked at the diagram. He looked at Mabel.

"I thought we were just building a greenhouse."

"We are," said Mabel. "But we're doing it without anyone shouting at anyone else. Ines says the screen helps."

"I'm not joining anything."

"It's a tablet, Sam. Nobody's asking you to marry it."

Hob arrived, carrying a flask. He sat at the end of the table and said nothing.

Mrs Kavanagh arrived at two minutes to eight, carrying the folder, and took the chair nearest the sandwiches with the air of a woman who intended to keep an eye on them. She had a pencil in her hand rather than the pen, which Mabel understood to mean she had not yet decided to disapprove.

Piotr arrived, wiping his boots on the mat. He looked at the diagram and nodded.

"Good roof angle," he said. "Who designed it?"

"Ines got it," said Sam. "She's on some sort of network."

Piotr looked at Ines.

"It's a group," she said. "Online. People who build things and share the plans."

"What kind of things?"

"Greenhouses. Sheds. Water systems. Anything, really."

Piotr considered this. He looked at the diagram again.

"It's a good roof angle," he said. "Whoever designed it knew what they were doing."

Gerard O'Callaghan came in last of the regulars and took the seat nearest the door, in the manner of a man who might need to leave. He did not take off his cap. Mrs Kavanagh glanced down at her folder and then up at him, and he looked at the ceiling.

"Windows," she said.

"I'm considering it."

She made a note. It was a short one.

Then a young lad from Skibbereen arrived.

Nobody knew who had told him about the gathering. He was nineteen or twenty, with a canvas bag over his shoulder and a notebook in his hand, and he stood in the doorway of the back room as if he wasn't sure he was allowed to be there.

"I heard about this," he said. "From the forum. I'm doing a course in sustainable agriculture. I wanted to see how you were doing it."

Nobody asked him to leave. Mabel looked at him for a moment, then nodded at the empty chair beside her.

"Sit down," she said. "You can hear the argument."

He sat down. He didn't say much for the rest of the evening. But he wrote things in his notebook, and he wrote them quickly.

Then O'Driscoll arrived.

Nobody had invited him. He stood in the doorway, arms crossed, with the expression of a man who had smelled something unpleasant and traced it to the room.

"What's this?" he asked. "A committee?"

"It's a gathering," said Mabel.

"Looks like a committee."

"It's a gathering, O'Driscoll. Sit down or leave."

He didn't sit down. He leaned against the wall and glowered. The wall was beside the sandwiches, which Mrs Kavanagh took as a provocation.

Ines stood up. She explained the diagram. She explained the Board. She pointed to the little gauge in the corner of the screen, a thin grey bar that had, at that moment, nothing to say.

"It's a thermometer," she said. "For how annoyed we are."

"How annoyed we are?" O'Driscoll repeated. "I'm already annoyed. Does that mean I win?"

"No," said Ines. "It means we keep talking."

The argument started almost immediately.

Sam wanted it by the road. "Easier to get materials in," he said. "You don't want to be dragging bags of cement across a field."

Piotr wanted it by the river. "Better drainage," he said. "More water for the plants."

"Rivers flood," said Sam.

"So raise the floor."

"You can't raise a floor on a promise."

O'Driscoll wanted it nowhere. "It's a waste of money," he said. "You'll spend a fortune on glass and it'll be wrecked by the first storm."

"Nobody's asked you to spend a cent," said Mabel.

"Somebody will."

Mrs Kavanagh asked what it would cost. Ines said she had a rough idea. Mrs Kavanagh said that a rough idea was a polite term for no idea, and the gauge on the tablet, which until then had been grey, turned the colour of a bruise and said ANNOYED.

"It's moved," said Sam.

"It's meant to," said Ines.

Gerard said it was a lot of glass. Nobody had mentioned the amount of glass. Piotr said that the roof angle would not be the problem; the problem would be somebody putting a ladder through it. Sam said that he had never in his life put a ladder through anything, and Hob, from the far end of the table, said "Lynch's shed," and Sam went quiet.

Mabel sat by the door, sipping her tea and watching the gauge creep upward. ANNOYED. VERY ANNOYED. EXTREMELY ANNOYED.

She let them argue for twenty minutes. Mrs Kavanagh had stopped writing. Ines's mother had come to the door with a fresh pot of tea, looked at the room, and gone away again without it.

Then Mabel stood up.

"Right," she said. "That's enough."

The room went quiet.

Mabel looked at the tablet. "Ines says the screen can break us into smaller groups. Groups that talk to each other, and then tell the rest. So let's do that. Sam, you go with Piotr. Work out the site, the foundations, the whole flood business. Hob, you go with Ines and see about the water. O'Driscoll, you're with me. We'll look at the glass."

"I'm not working with anyone," said O'Driscoll.

"Then go home," said Mabel. "But if you stay, you're helping."

She was not chairing. She had told several people so. Nobody pointed out that she was now standing at the head of the table with her cardigan sleeves pushed up, assigning people to rooms.

O'Driscoll stayed.

They broke into groups. Sam and Piotr went out to look at the site with a torch and a tape measure. Hob and Ines took the diagram to the corner of the room and bent over the rainwater arrows with the seriousness of two people defusing something. Mrs Kavanagh remained where she was, with the sandwiches, doing sums. The lad from Skibbereen went with Sam and Piotr. He didn't say anything. He just listened, and held the torch.

Mabel and O'Driscoll sat at the corner table and looked at the diagram of the glass panels.

"That's too thin," said O'Driscoll, pointing.

"It's what we can afford."

"It'll break in the wind. You want at least six millimetres."

"And where would we get six millimetres?"

O'Driscoll folded his arms.

"I'm not telling you where to get it."

"Nobody asked."

"Good."

Mabel held out her hand without a word. Mrs Kavanagh, across the room, appeared to feel it. She took the red pen out of her pocket, looked at it for some time, then rose and carried it across the room herself, handing it over like a woman releasing a hostage. Mabel wrote 6mm on the back of her hand and returned it, and neither of them said anything about it, and O'Driscoll watched all of this with an expression of deep and growing suspicion.

When the groups came back to the table, something unexpected had happened.

Sam had agreed that the river offered better long-term water access, provided they raised the floor. Piotr had agreed to draw up the plans for a raised foundation. Hob had a way of collecting rainwater that did not involve any of the things Ines had drawn, and Ines had crossed out her arrows without being asked. Mabel said that the glass was thinner than anyone liked, and that this was a worry, and that it would go in the Notebook. O'Driscoll said nothing. He had said six millimetres once, and did not intend to be drawn on it.

The gauge dropped. VERY ANNOYED. Then, after a moment, ANNOYED.

"That's as low as it goes," said Ines. "It's the village's resting state."

Ines put the proposal on the Board.

It was a plain proposal. It said that a greenhouse would be built beside the river, on a raised foundation, to Ines's design, with the glass to be sourced locally if possible. It said that people would sign up for what they could do. It did not say who would pay.

The gauge changed. The thin grey bar dissolved, and the screen showed the proposal on its own, with a single question underneath.

"Any principled objections?" said Ines.

Nobody spoke. Somebody's chair creaked.

A small red square appeared in the corner of the screen. It was not a large red square. Whoever had designed the tablet had apparently thought it was enough.

"That's me," said Mrs Kavanagh.

The room turned.

"I've an objection." She put the pencil down. "We're proposing to build a greenhouse on land that belongs to the council, on the strength of a conversation at a sugar bowl. I can live with a great deal. I can't live with that."

"It's a strip of grass," said Sam.

"It's a strip of grass that belongs to somebody else. If we put a greenhouse on it and they say no, we've a great many panes of glass and nowhere to put them."

"So what do you want?"

"A licence. In writing. Before a single thing is built."

"And who'd hold it?" said Ines. She did not say it as a challenge. She wanted to know.

Mrs Kavanagh took a moment.

"Not any of us," she said. "If it's in Sam's name, it's Sam's. If it's in mine, God help us. It should be Odd Jobs."

Ines typed it in. The proposal changed. The sentence about the river was followed by another sentence about the licence, and the licence was held by Ballywobble Odd Jobs, and nothing was to be built until it had been signed. The red square flickered, as if it had been about to say something else, and went out.

"Any principled objections?" said Ines.

O'Driscoll had been watching all of this from the wall. He had watched the red square arrive and he had watched it go. Something had happened in the room that he had not been expecting. A woman had said she could not live with something, and the room had simply stopped and changed what it was doing.

"What's the difference," he said, "between an objection and an opinion?"

Ines looked at him, and she took the question seriously, which was part of why people forgave her for the look.

"An opinion is something you'd like changed. An objection is something you can't live with."

"And if I can't live with it?"

"Then we don't do it. We change the proposal."

"Even if it's only me?"

"Even if it's only you."

O'Driscoll thought about it. He had a great many opinions. He wasn't sure he had any objections.

"I can live with it," he said.

The gauge read AGREED.

Nobody had won.

Everyone could live with it, which is the highest compliment in politics.

"Where did the plans come from?" asked Mr Pennyfeather, two years later, when he eventually visited.

"Ines," said Sam.

"Ines drew them?"

"No. Ines got them." Sam thought about how to explain it. "She's on some sort of... I don't know what you'd call it. A network. Of people who build things. She asks a question, and somebody somewhere answers it. Could be someone in another county. Could be someone in another country. She found the roof angle from a group in Portugal. The rainwater system came from a man in Wales. The ventilation was someone in Canada."

Pennyfeather looked at him.

"And she pays for this?"

"No."

"Then why do they do it?"

Sam shrugged.

"Because she asks. And because they ask her things back."

Pennyfeather wrote something down. He crossed it out. He wrote it down again.

"That sounds rather like a community," he said.

"It is," said Sam. "It's just not one you can see from the road."

The Notebook recorded the materials, energy, labour and environmental costs of each design. Mrs Kavanagh kept the euros in her folder, where they could not be influenced by environmental considerations.

The first design to be marked in red was the most beautiful one. It had a cupola. It had come from Canada with a note that said You will love this, and Piotr had held it at arm's length for a full minute and then said, quietly, that it was the loveliest thing he had ever seen drawn, and that it would need to be glazed on nine faces.

"Who wanted the cupola?" said Mabel.

"Nobody," said Sam. "It was on the plans."

A beautiful design that required too much glass, timber or maintenance was marked in red. A less impressive design that could be repaired with locally available materials might be preferable. The village spent several evenings making the greenhouse less greedy, and by the end of them it was a plain, long, sensible thing with a pitched roof, and nobody could look at it without wanting to take the cupola back.

Building began the following Monday.

The Rota took over.

The Rota was the nearest thing Ballywobble had to a manager, and it wasn't a person.

It was a list.

It went up in the porch of the old chapel, on a sheet of lining paper, in Ines's handwriting and Mrs Kavanagh's ruler. It had columns for frames, glazing, soil, water, foundations and someone-to-make-the-tea.

The last was considered a load-bearing activity.

People wrote their names where they could. Those who couldn't hold a hammer straight put their names down for carrying things, preparing meals or minding children. Mabel's name appeared under tea on every day of the first fortnight, in a hand so firm it had gone through the paper. Nobody had asked her to. Nobody suggested she might need a break.

When they needed glass that wasn't available locally, a temporary group was formed to organise transport from the next valley. Sam had a trailer. Gerard, after a long silence, turned out to have a second one. When the glass arrived, the group dissolved. Nobody needed to appoint a permanent glass transportation manager.

Two years on, Mr Pennyfeather would watch Hob coordinate eleven people and a cart without raising his voice.

"How do you do that?"

"I don't," said Hob. "It's on the Rota. I just read it out loud."

On the night of the gathering, long after the others had gone, the lad from Skibbereen stayed behind to stack the chairs.

He didn't say much. He stacked them carefully, one on top of the other, making sure they were even. Ines noticed that he did it the way someone does a thing they've been taught to do properly.

"You didn't have to stay," she said.

"I know."

He put another chair on the stack.

"I'm Ines," she said.

"Donal."

They shook hands. His hand was cold from the rain outside.

"You said you're doing a course," Ines said. "In sustainable agriculture."

"Second year. In Clonakilty."

"What do you think of it?"

He considered the question seriously.

"Half of it's grand," he said. "The other half is fellas from Dublin telling us how to spread slurry."

Ines laughed.

"That's about right," she said.

They stacked the last of the chairs. The back room of The Prassa was quiet now, and the lights were low, and Ines's mother was wiping down the counter in the next room.

"What you were saying about the plans," Donal said. "The network. Where did you find it?"

"A forum. It's called the Integral Site. It's mostly people who build things and share what they've learned."

"Is it any good?"

"It's the best thing I've found. There's a woman in Portugal who's been doing greenhouses for thirty years. A man in Wales who does water systems. A group in Canada who've worked out how to heat a greenhouse without electricity."

Donal looked at her.

"And they just tell you?"

"They just tell me. And I tell them things back. It's not a transaction. It's just what people do when they're interested in the same things."

He nodded slowly.

"I've been looking for something like that," he said. "For the course. There's nothing in the library that isn't twenty years old."

Ines took out her notebook. She wrote down the name of the forum, tore out the page, and handed it to him.

"Start there," she said. "Don't be shy. They're used to people asking questions."

He looked at the page.

"Thanks."

"You'll have to introduce yourself. They don't like lurkers."

"What's a lurker?"

"Someone who reads but never says anything."

Donal considered this.

"I'll introduce myself," he said.

He folded the page carefully and put it in his pocket. Then he picked up his canvas bag and his notebook.

"I might come back," he said. "If that's all right."

"It's a free country," said Ines.

He smiled—the first real smile she'd seen from him—and went out into the rain.

Ines watched him go. Then she took out her tablet, opened the page she had bookmarked months ago—the one with the village analogy, the bicycle, the ledger that wasn't a ledger—and read it again. She had read it so often she could have recited it. She still read it again.

Then she finished stacking the chairs, and turned off the lights, and went to help her mother close up.
