Chapter Nine

The Visitors

By the second year, people from other villages had started visiting.

Some came out of curiosity.

Some came because they were interested in the greenhouse.

Others came because they had heard about the Ledger and wanted to know how it worked.

A group from a town twenty miles away arrived one Saturday morning.

They were particularly interested in the Board.

One of them, a man called Andrew, spent most of the morning asking who was in charge.

"Nobody," said Ines.

"That can't be right."

"Why?"

"Because somebody has to make decisions."

"They do."

"Who?"

"Everyone, depending on the decision."

Andrew looked unconvinced.

He had spent fifteen years working in local government and had learned that every decision needed a responsible person, a budget code and a meeting in which everyone agreed that another meeting was required.

He was not accustomed to the idea that people might simply organise themselves around a shared problem.

The visitors also asked about the Ledger.

They wanted to know how it would work in a town of three thousand people.

Nobody had a complete answer.

That evening, Ines admitted to Mabel that she was worried.

"What if it doesn't scale?"

Mabel was sorting potatoes.

"What's that mean?"

"What if it works here but doesn't work somewhere bigger?"

"Then they'll have to work it out."

"But shouldn't we have a model?"

Mabel considered this.

Ines had the tablet open on the table, face up, the way she always had it when she was thinking. She had been reading the same page for a week—the one about nodes and networks, about what happens when a thing that works in one place meets a place that is not the same place. She had not shown it to anyone. She was not sure it helped.

"There's no point building a bigger greenhouse if you haven't worked out how to water the first one," Mabel said.

Ines laughed.

Mabel continued sorting.

"Besides, love, we're not building a religion. If something doesn't work, change it."

Ines turned the tablet face down. She had read enough for one evening.

It was Gerard who noticed, in the end.

He had been helping to sort the larder—which was to say, he had been standing in the corner, criticising the way the shelves were organised, until Mabel had told him to help or leave. He had chosen to help, in his own way, and he had been sorting jars of preserved fruit into rows by size.

The Ledger was open on the table where Ines had left it.

He read down the column. Most of the names were familiar. Sam. Ines. Mabel. His own name, which surprised him. And then, near the bottom, he saw:

Hob — declined.

He read it twice.

Then he found Mabel.

"Why won't he sign?" he asked.

"He doesn't hold with it."

"Why not?"

Mabel told him. Gerard listened. He didn't interrupt. When she had finished, he was quiet for a long time.

"I know what he means," he said.

Mabel looked at him.

"I've got three sheds full of things I'll never use," Gerard said. "Because I was afraid of what would happen if I didn't have them. Because I'd seen what happens when you can't pay for something and you have to ask." He looked at the Ledger. "This isn't that."

"No," said Mabel. "It isn't."

Gerard was quiet for a moment.

"I'll talk to him," he said.

He found Hob the following Tuesday, in the shed, fixing a kettle.

It was a wet afternoon, and the shed was warm, and the kettle was the sort that could be fixed if you had the right screwdriver and the patience. Hob had both. He had taken the base off and laid the parts out on a cloth in front of him, in the order he would need them.

Gerard stood in the doorway for a while and watched.

"You're busy," he said.

Hob didn't look up. "I'm fixing a kettle."

"I can see that."

He came in and sat down on an upturned bucket. The shed smelled of cold metal and sawdust and the faint, sharp tang of the kettle's insides.

"I've been thinking about the Ledger," Gerard said.

Hob said nothing.

"I noticed your name in the declined column."

Hob put down the screwdriver. He didn't look at Gerard. He looked at the kettle.

"I noticed you didn't sign," Gerard said.

"No," said Hob. "I didn't."

"Mabel told me why."

Hob was quiet for a moment.

"Did she?"

"She did."

Gerard picked up one of the pieces of the kettle—a small brass screw—and turned it over in his fingers.

"I've got three sheds full of stuff," he said. "Nails. Screws. Timber. Tractor parts. Things I've been keeping for forty years." He put the screw down. "I don't need any of it. I've never needed any of it. But I've been keeping it anyway, because I was afraid of what would happen if I didn't have it. Because I was afraid of being caught short."

Hob looked at him.

"I know what that's like," Hob said.

"I know you do."

They sat in silence for a while. The rain drummed on the roof of the shed.

"The thing is," Gerard said, "the Ledger isn't money. It's not a debt. It's just a record of what people did. And the credits fade. They're not a claim on anyone." He looked at Hob. "It's not the thing you're afraid of."

Hob said nothing.

"I'm not telling you to sign," Gerard said. "I'm just telling you what I've seen. You can do what you like with it."

He stood up.

"I'll leave you to the kettle."

He was at the door when Hob spoke.

"Gerard."

Gerard turned.

"Why did you stop hoarding?"

Gerard thought about it.

"I didn't stop," he said. "I still keep my own tins. But I started giving things away, and nothing bad happened. Nobody took advantage. Nobody laughed at me. The world didn't end." He shrugged. "I suppose I got tired of being afraid."

Hob looked at the kettle for a long moment.

"I'll think about it," he said.

Gerard nodded.

"That's all I'm asking."

The following Thursday, Hob came to the shed.

He stood at the back, as he usually did. He didn't say anything. When Ines brought out the Ledger and began to go through the entries, he waited until she was finished.

Then he said, quietly, "I'll sign."

Ines looked at him.

"You don't have to."

"I know."

She gave him the pen. He signed his name—Robert Hobbs, in a small, careful hand—and then he handed the pen back and went to put the kettle on.

Nobody made a fuss. That was the point.

But Mabel noticed that Gerard was smiling, and that he was trying very hard not to be seen doing it.

That evening, Aoife came to find Ines.

She had a printout in her hand. It was a screenshot of a thread, and she put it on the table between them without saying anything.

The title was: The Ballywobble shed has a 'face' and she's 34.

Ines read it. It named Aoife as the person who had made the flyer, the person who answered the phone, the person who was, in the words of the thread, "the only one with a name attached to anything".

"Who wrote it?" Ines asked.

"Nobody I know," said Aoife. "That's not the point."

She sat down.

"The point is, they're right. I made the flyer. I put my name on it. I'm the only one who answers the phone. If someone wants to complain about the Shed, they complain to me."

"We can change that," said Ines.

"I know."

They sat in silence for a moment.

"I'll ask Piotr and Tunde," Aoife said. "They're already there every week. They can share the phone."

Ines looked at her.

"You're not upset?"

"I'm furious," said Aoife. "But not at the thread. At myself. I made the flyer because I wanted people to know about us. I didn't think about what it would mean to be the only name on it."

She folded the printout and put it in her pocket.

"It'll be better with three names," she said. "Less interesting."

"Less interesting is good," said Ines.

"Yes," said Aoife. "That's what I'm learning."

Two weeks later, the Shed pinned a second note beside the ownership page on the chapel wall. It was headed THE LEDGER — WHAT IT IS AND WHAT IT ISN'T, and it was two paragraphs long. It said what the Ledger recorded, what it did not record, that it was not money, that it could not be exchanged for money, and that anyone who wanted to see it could ask Ines or Donal and they would bring it to the chapel on a Thursday.

It was not exciting. It was not ideological. It was the kind of document that would make a suspicious person less suspicious and a determined person more determined.

Hob read it. He read it twice, and then he said, to nobody in particular, "That's fair enough," which from Hob was practically a speech.

Gerard read it. He didn't say anything, but the following week he brought another bag of nails to the repair evening, which was his way of agreeing with things.

O'Driscoll read it. He sent Sam a note about a supplier for the second greenhouse's glass, which Sam took to mean he had read it and approved.

Barry G. did not read it. He had already moved on to a thread about the price of diesel.
